### Aerodynamic flow behavior

* *Aero devices slide*  
* Aero devices on the car  
  * **Spoiler** \- blade shaped body panel fixed to the rear of a vehicle, usually across the back of the trunk (or deck lid) area  
    * Disrupts or “spoils” air that would otherwise flow over the truck area and down the back of the car  
    * Stops generation of lift force (like would happen with air flowing over an airplane wing)  
    * The angle and hight of the spoiler can be adjusted  
  * **Splitter** \- flat surface that protrudes from the front fascia of the car roughly parallel with the ground  
    * Creates a shelf separating high pressure air hitting the front of the car from low pressure, high velocity air moving under the car  
  * **Wing** \- structure emanating from the body of the car which air can flow both over and under to generate downforce  
    * Can be located on the front or rear of the car  
    * Likes like upside-down airplane wing (create high pressure above and low pressure below) and generate downforce  
  * **Diffuser** \- upward sloping section at the rear of a car’s underbody which accelerates air flow to create a low pressure zone that sucks the car to the ground  
* Ground effect  
  * *Ground effect slide*  
  * **Ground effect** \- the aerodynamic exploitation of the close physical proximity between a car's underbody and the track surface.  
  * The idea of ground effect relies on a few aerodynamic principals that allow us to make highly efficient (low drag penalty) downforce  
  * **Bernoulli Principle** \- when a fluid (like air) is forced through a constricted space, its velocity increases, and its pressure drops   
    * To the extent allowed by the rule set, we design the underbody of our car to exploit this principal  
    * Generally done by shaping the underbody like an inverted funnel that constricts in the middle and then diverges  
      * Air rushes underneath the low nose, accelerates rapidly through the narrowest part of the floor creating low pressure   
      * Low pressure zone under the car and high atmospheric pressure above pushes the car down  
  * *Paper tent experiment*  
* Flow separation vs attached flow  
  * *Flow separation vs attached flow slide*  
  * To generate efficient downforce, we must try to control how the air sticks to the surfaces of the body  
  * **Attached flow** \- when air molecules smoothly follow the contours of the car’s body. Attached flow is predictable, and highly efficient.  
    * For aero devices like a wing to work correctly the airflow across its surfaces must remain attached  
  * **Flow separation** \- when air molecules detach from a car's bodywork, turbulent flow is created   
    * This can happen due to sharp changes in body surfaces  
    * Limits effectiveness of aero devices on the car further down stream  
    * Creates pressure drag (low pressure behind the car physically pulling it back) which hurts straight-line performance.  
  * We can use pressure taps and flow visualization paint (flow-vis for short) to understand flow behavior on our car  
* Traffic (from other cars)  
  * *Aero traffic slide*  
  * **Traffic** \- Often our race car will operate in the turbulent “dirty” wake of another race car and we should understand how this impacts balance and performance  
  * Some general principles about running in traffic are intuitive  
    * Following another car in a straight line is generally good   
      * Front car “breaks” the air allowing following cars to move through slightly lower pressure air which pulls the car forward (called slipstream or draft)  
      * The magnitude of this effect varies greatly depending on the type of race car  
    * Following another car while cornering is generally bad   
      * Our car generates less downforce due to turbulent, lower pressure air and thus reduces our ability to corner significantly  
  * It is almost always preferable to run in “clean” air  
    * The benefit from slipstream on the straights does not overcome the reduction in cornering capability  
    * This has a significant impact on how we call strategy during the race (i.e. track position after pitting)   
  * Clean air may not be preferable in a power limited rather than traction limited scenario   
    * Ironically, two examples of this are the Daytona 500 and Indianapolis 500   
  * Wind tunnels and CFD simulation, do not model traffic well  
    * Often have to regress aero behaviors due to traffic from on-track data  
    * May also have our racing driver provide feedback in a DiL (driver in loop) simulator on how dramatic the effect of traffic should be
