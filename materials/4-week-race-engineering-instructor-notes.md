* ## Course Prerequisites

  * ### Coordinate systems

    * *Coordinate systems slide*  
    * **Cartesian coordinate system**  
      * Method which allows for locating points in space  
      * Uses an origin (0, 0, 0\) and 3 axis system (with signed directions)  
      * Motorsports engineering nomenclature  
        * X axis \| longitudinal (fore/aft) \| roll (rotation about)  
        * Y axis \| lateral (left/right) \| pitch (rotation about)  
        * Z axis \| vertical (up and down) \| yaw (rotation about)  
    * Coordinate systems allow us to know:  
      * Where points of interest are relative to each other on the car (chassis frame)  
      * Height of certain points relative to the ground (ground frame)  
      * Where the car or ground is relative to anything else (global frame)  
    * Course mostly focuses on chassis and ground frame

  * ### Unit and sign conventions

    * Must use a common convention to effectively utilize a coordinate system and communicate about any object with features (like a race car)  
    * *Units and sign conventions slide*  
    * Two main conventions: SAE vs ISO  
      * **SAE** \- Society of Automotive Engineers  
      * **ISO** \- International Organization for Standardization  
      * Differences  
        * \+Z, SAE is down, ISO is up  
        * \+Y, SAE is driver’s right, ISO is driver’s left  
      * Similarities  
        * \+X is forward  
        * Both follow right hand rule  
    * Racing series may use SAE, ISO, or their own convention

  * ### Motorsport engineering terminology

    * *Motorsports terminology slide*  
    * There are terms in motorsports which describe performance or characteristics of any pair of tires and/or associated suspension  
      * Front / rear axle \- refers to front or rear pair   
        * Abbreviated as F and R  
        * Does not refer to a mechanical axle on the car  
      * Left / right side \- refers to left or right pair  
        * Abbreviated as LS or L, and RS or R  
    * The term “corner” is commonly used for an individual tire and/or associated suspension  
      * Abbreviated as FL, FR, RL, RR or LF, RF, LR, RR  
    * Vertical movement (**heave**)  
      * Car’s vertical movement relative to the ground is called heave  
        * Slang terms: travel or bump/droop

  * ### Units

    * Similar to coordinate system conventions, we need to use a common unit system  
    * Two main unit system: SI vs Imperial  
      * SI \- International System of Units (metric system)  
      * Imperial \- system used in USA  
    * Most racing series use SI, NASCAR uses imperial, IndyCar can be mixed  
    * *Pass around props for different units (mm, inch, N, lbf)*

  * ### Cornering and straightline physics

    * *Cornering and straightline physics slide*  
    * Will use free body diagrams to help us understand straightline and cornering forces  
    * **Center of gravity** (CofG)  
      * Exact virtual balance point of the car's mass in all three dimensions   
      * Theoretical point where the entire vehicle’s weight is concentrated and through which all external gravitational and inertial forces act  
      * Lower is better; limits sprung body movement and reduces lateral load transfer (more on this later)  
    * Vertical forces  
      * Weight and aerodynamic downforce balanced by track normal forces  
    * Lateral forces  
      * Centrifugal force balanced by tire cornering forces  
    * Longitudinal forces  
      * Aerodynamic drag, tire rolling resistance, and inertial force balanced by thrust (from power unit) and braking force  
    * If forces in the same plane on either side of the CofG are not balanced we create a moment about the CofG  
    * Constant speed (or stopped) if sum of forces in any direction is equal  
    * Accelerating if forces are not equal

  * ### Basic balance theory

    * *Basic balance theory slide*  
    * Covering at a high level here will re-visit in more detail later  
    * Types of balance  
      * **Understeer** (tight) – front tires slipping (less lateral capability) more than rears \- stable but can be slow  
      * **Oversteer** (loose) – rear tires slipping more than fronts \- less stable but potentially faster  
      * Neutral \- neither understeer or understeer  
    * Race engineer wants to know about balance from the driver ASAP once the car is on track, and will ask frequently if balance has changed  
    * Need this information to start thinking about the next changes  
    * Bottoming and “ride” are other common questions  
    * Common for driver to use number scale (1 to 10\) and organize feedback by corner segment  
    * Mechanical setup and aerodynamic properties of the car have biggest effect on balance

  * ### Wheel alignment	

    * *Wheel alignment slide*  
    * **Camber** \- Angle between a tilted wheel plane and the vertical (Z) axis  
      * Unit of degrees (SI unit of radians)  
    * **Toe** \- Direction the wheel plane points relative to the longitudinal (X) axis  
    * **Caster**\- Angle between steering axis (straight line between outer upper and lower control arm pivots) and the lateral (Y) axis.   
      * Causes a self centering effect since the contact patch is behind the pivot.

* ## Tires

  * ### What do tires do? How do they work?

    * *Tire intro slide*  
    * Tires generate forces and torques (moments) in the ground plane (at the **contact patch**) to allow for cornering, front drive, braking, and stability  
    * Support the vehicle’s weight and all vertical forces imposed on the vehicle (aerodynamic downforce)  
    * Tire behavior is highly non-linear, extremely complex, and not fully understood  
    * We will limit focus to; drive (or longitudinal) forces, cornering (or lateral) forces, effect of vertical load on the tires, and key variables that influence tire behavior  
    * Play video: [Tire Test Systems](https://youtu.be/F-SukU5848g?si=pcecaz312LWF2dd5&t=39)

  * ### Drive (longitudinal) force generation

    * *Lateral tire force slide*  
    * How are longitudinal forces generated?  
      * A driving torque is applied to the tire by the vehicle’s drivetrain  
      * When the tire starts spinning slightly faster than the vehicle is traveling, longitudinal shear forces are generated at the contact patch  
      * **Slip ratio** \- proportional difference between the rotational speed of the tire and forward speed of the car  
      * For a given tire and vehicle setup, there is a slip ratio which produces the most longitudinal force  
    * Play video: [NHRA Drag Race Tire Wrinkle in Slow Motion \| Top Fuel and Funny Car](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gXp2QgY1OB8)

  * ### Cornering (lateral) force generation

    * *Lateral tire force slide*  
    * How are lateral forces generated?  
      * The driver steers the wheel  
      * The tire does not instantly follow the new direction; its tread and carcass deform laterally  
      * **Slip angle** \- angular difference between the direction a tire is pointing and the actual direction it is traveling  
      * Tread develops shear forces against the pavement  
      * The summed shear forces act sideways on the tire, producing lateral force (Fy) that allows the car to corner  
    * *Slip disc tire experiment*

  * ### Effect of slip angle

    * *Cornering stiffness plot in data viewer*  
    * **Cornering stiffness** \- linear region of lateral force vs slip angle curve  
    * Lateral force builds linearly with increasing slip angle it becomes non-linear  
    * Peaks at a certain slip angle then starts falling off  
    * How quickly this fall off happens is incredibly important to how “on the limit” the driver can keep the car.  
    * *Review different slip angle plots in data viewer*

  * ### Effect of vertical loading

    * *Different Fz load cornering stiffness plots in data viewer*  
    * Increase in Fz generates more Fy, but at a decreasing rate (important concept)  
      * *Example calculation for lateral load transfer cornering force*  
    * This increase also moves the slip angle at which peak Fy occurs  
    * What else happens with more Fz?  
      * **Loaded radius** \- height from the ground to the center of the wheel \- gets smaller as the side wall deflects

  * ### Effect of camber

    * *Camber contact patch slide*  
    * Why do we want negative camber on a road course, and negative camber on the outside tires with positive camber on the inside tires on an oval?  
      * Larger contact patch during cornering  
      * **Camber thrust** \- force generated from the tire due to the tire leaning into the corner creating additional shear force  
    * Why can’t we run large camber all the time?  
      * Accelerates wear (ride only on the inside rim of the tire down the straights) \- might run higher amounts for shorter events (like qualifying)  
      * Reduces the contact patch during straight-line acceleration \- typically run lower camber angles in the rear since most race cars are rear wheel drive  
    * *Different camber angle corner stiffness plots in data viewer*

  * ### Effect of pressure

    * *Different tire pressures in plots in data viewer*  
    * Changes the characteristics of how Fy builds with increasing Fz  
    * Why is this important? We need to understand how much load is going to be on the car for a given event

  * ### Effect of temperature

    * *Effect of tire temp slide*  
    * Temperature (temp)  
      * Tires are designed to operate optimally in a certain temp range  
      * Temp greatly impacts molecular adhesion between track and contact patch  
        * A hot tire off a race car is literally sticky to the touch  
      * Changing temp results in charging internal pressure and thus behavior  
        * Will typically start tire pressures low so they build to desired operating pressure  
      * Brakes are a major source of heat build in a tire

  * ### Fundamental learnings

    * *Fundamental tire learnings slide*  
    * Tires have very non-linear behavior  
      * More camber good to a certain point  
      * More slip angle good to a certain point  
    * Sum of shear forces developed at the contact patch is how tires generate lateral and longitudinal forces  
    * We want high, constant vertical load on the tire  
      * The more vertical load on the tire, the more lateral force it can generate  
      * Fy/Fz curve again non linear which means we need to limit lateral load transfer when cornering  
    * How do we achieve high load on the tires?  
      * Aerodynamic load

* ## Aerodynamics \- “Aero”

  * ### Intro

    * *Aerodynamics intro slide*  
    * Why are tires and aero first order concerns when it comes to designing or setting up a race car?   
      * Because in the most general sense, we are moving a solid body through a fluid while (generally) in contact with a solid surface  
      * In engineering, we need to initially think about problems at a very basic level.   
    * We could answer, “because we are moving a car on a paved surface through air”, but describing it more generally allows us to consider problems we will face without pre-conceived restrictions  
    * **Aerodynamics** \- the study of how air moves and the forces it exerts on solid objects moving through it  
    * The better we understand how the air flows over, under, and around our particular racecar, the more we can optimize it for performance  
    * **Aero platform** \- We can learn how to position our car relative to the air and track surface to optimize aerodynamic performance (similar to tires)  
    * Aero forces are the largest source of vertical load for our tires and the biggest restriction on how fast we can go in a straight-line (drag)

  * ### Forces & moment nomenclature

    * *Aero force and moment slide*  
    * Drag and thrust (Fx, longitudinal, CD)  
      * Force from air that acts in the opposite direction of the heading of the vehicle is called **drag**  
      * Where do we care most about drag?....in a straight-line  
        * Pushing air out of the way requires a lot of power   
        * Drag is the primary limiting factor for a race car's top speed, always want less drag  
      * Fx that acts in the same direction we are heading is:  
        * Good in a straight-line  
        * Can be upsetting to the car as it changes direction (like on corner entry or exit).  
    * Side Force (Fy, lateral, CS)  
      * **Side force** \- Force from air which acts perpendicular to the car’s direction  
      * When do we generate side force?....when the vehicle goes into yaw (car is angled relative to the oncoming airflow)  
      * Side force can have a stabilizing effect so long as it is not due to wind  
    * Lift and downforce (Fz, vertical, CL)  
      * When air flowing over, under or around the car generates downward force, we call it **downforce**   
      * We always want as much downforce as we can get  
        * In what scenario is lift a big concern?.....when a car spins around backwards (fly into air, roof flaps).  
      * The cost of downforce is drag  
        * **L over D (CL/CD)** \- Trade off between downforce and drag is a key concept in all forms of auto racing  
    * Aero moments (Mx, My, Mz)  
      * When aerodynamic forces exerted by the air on the car are not “balanced” about the CofG, aero moments are created  
      * Pitch moment (My, CPM) \- difference in Fz (downforce) on the front and rear axle. Largest impact on balance.  
      * Roll moment (Mx, CRM) \- difference in Fz (downforce) between the left and right sides. Not super concerned about roll moment (cars are generally symmetrical).  
      * Yaw moment (My, CYM) \- difference in Fy (side force) on the front and rear axles. This impacts stability and promotes yawing behavior.  
    * Quantifying aerodynamic behavior  
      * **Count** \- We typically quantify aerodynamic forces using dimensionless coefficients called counts (CD, CL, CLF, CLR, CS, CPM, CRM, CYM).  
        * Drag counts  
          * F1: 1.5  
          * NASCAR Cup: 0.4  
          * IndyCar: 1.4  
        * Downforce counts  
          * F1: \-6.0  
          * NASCAR Cup: \-1.0  
          * IndyCar: \-4.5  
      * Allows us to compare aerodynamic efficiency of different shapes and vehicles regardless of the car's speed or size.

  * ### Aerodynamic force behavior

    * *Aero force equations slide*  
    * Force equations  
      * Force from Drag  \= ½ \* density \* velocity (of car) \* drag coefficient \* frontal area (of car)  
      * Downforce \= ½ \* density \* velocity (of car) \* lift coefficient \* frontal area (of car)  
        * Which term dominates?....velocity (squared term)  
        * If you 3x your speed, you 9x drag/downforce  
      * Because aero forces grow quadratically with speed, balance can change completely between low-speed and high-speed corners  
    * *Center of pressure slide*  
    * Center of pressure (CoP)  
      * **Center of pressure (CoP)** \- the single theoretical point where all aerodynamic downforce forces sum together  
      * Analogous to CofG where masses balance   
      * **Aero balance** \- distribution of aero forces between front and rear of a car and their subsequent impact on balance  
        * If the CoP is too far forward, the car will tend to oversteer at high-speeds  
        * If the CoP is too far back, the car will understeer at high speeds.  
        * Aero balance is typically expressed as a ratio of front lift force over total lift force

  * ### Aerodynamic flow behavior

    * *Aero devices slide*  
    * Aero devices on the car  
      * **Spoiler** \- blade shaped body panel fixed to the rear of a vehicle, usually across the back of the trunk (or deck lid) area  
        * Disrupts or “spoils” air that would otherwise flow over the truck area and down the back of the car  
        * Stops generation of lift force (like would happen with air flowing over an airplane wing)  
        * The angle and hight of the spoiler can be adjusted  
      * **Splitter** \- flat surface that protrudes from the front fascia of the car roughly parallel with the ground  
        * Creates a shelf separating high pressure air hitting the front of the car from low pressure, high velocity air moving under the car  
      * **Wing** \- structure emanating from the body of the car which air can flow both over and under to generate downforce  
        * Can be located on the front or rear of the car  
        * Likes like upside-down airplane wing (create high pressure above and low pressure below) and generate downforce  
      * **Diffuser** \- upward sloping section at the rear of a car’s underbody which accelerates air flow to create a low pressure zone that sucks the car to the ground  
    * Ground effect  
      * *Ground effect slide*  
      * **Ground effect** \- the aerodynamic exploitation of the close physical proximity between a car's underbody and the track surface.  
      * The idea of ground effect relies on a few aerodynamic principals that allow us to make highly efficient (low drag penalty) downforce  
      * **Bernoulli Principle** \- when a fluid (like air) is forced through a constricted space, its velocity increases, and its pressure drops   
        * To the extent allowed by the rule set, we design the underbody of our car to exploit this principal  
        * Generally done by shaping the underbody like an inverted funnel that constricts in the middle and then diverges  
          * Air rushes underneath the low nose, accelerates rapidly through the narrowest part of the floor creating low pressure   
          * Low pressure zone under the car and high atmospheric pressure above pushes the car down  
      * *Paper tent experiment*  
    * Flow separation vs attached flow  
      * *Flow separation vs attached flow slide*  
      * To generate efficient downforce, we must try to control how the air sticks to the surfaces of the body  
      * **Attached flow** \- when air molecules smoothly follow the contours of the car’s body. Attached flow is predictable, and highly efficient.  
        * For aero devices like a wing to work correctly the airflow across its surfaces must remain attached  
      * **Flow separation** \- when air molecules detach from a car's bodywork, turbulent flow is created   
        * This can happen due to sharp changes in body surfaces  
        * Limits effectiveness of aero devices on the car further down stream  
        * Creates pressure drag (low pressure behind the car physically pulling it back) which hurts straight-line performance.  
      * We can use pressure taps and flow visualization paint (flow-vis for short) to understand flow behavior on our car  
    * Traffic (from other cars)  
      * *Aero traffic slide*  
      * **Traffic** \- Often our race car will operate in the turbulent “dirty” wake of another race car and we should understand how this impacts balance and performance  
      * Some general principles about running in traffic are intuitive  
        * Following another car in a straight line is generally good   
          * Front car “breaks” the air allowing following cars to move through slightly lower pressure air which pulls the car forward (called slipstream or draft)  
          * The magnitude of this effect varies greatly depending on the type of race car  
        * Following another car while cornering is generally bad   
          * Our car generates less downforce due to turbulent, lower pressure air and thus reduces our ability to corner significantly  
      * It is almost always preferable to run in “clean” air  
        * The benefit from slipstream on the straights does not overcome the reduction in cornering capability  
        * This has a significant impact on how we call strategy during the race (i.e. track position after pitting)   
      * Clean air may not be preferable in a power limited rather than traction limited scenario   
        * Ironically, two examples of this are the Daytona 500 and Indianapolis 500   
      * Wind tunnels and CFD simulation, do not model traffic well  
        * Often have to regress aero behaviors due to traffic from on-track data  
        * May also have our racing driver provide feedback in a DiL (driver in loop) simulator on how dramatic the effect of traffic should be

  * ### Influence of weather

    * *Aero weather slide*  
    * Weather has a large effect on air density and thus aero performance  
      * Dense air  
        * Caused by colder, dryer, and lower altitude conditions  
        * Results in more downforce, drag, and engine poser  
      * Thinner air  
        * Caused by hotter, more humid, and higher altitude conditions  
        * Results in less downforce, drag, and engineer power  
          * Less cornering grip but higher straightline speeds  
    * Wind is important but tricky to manage as its magnitude and direction can change quickly (Vegas is a great example)  
      * Gust of wind on corner entry or exit can upset the driver  
      * All we can do for wind is understand its potential impact and build in sufficient setup balance stability to accommodate

  * ### Fundamental learnings

    * *Fundamental aero learnings slide*  
    * Aerodynamic behavior is highly non-linear and hard to fully understand  
    * Aero forces scale quadratically with speed  
    * We want as much downforce (CL) as we can get for as little penalty in drag (CD) \- maximize L/D  
    * Aero force contribution to balance changes drastically with speed \- more contribution at high speeds  
    * Aero forces are the main source of vertical load on the car  
    * We need to understand our car's aerodynamic behavior so we can optimize our setup (aero platform)

* ## Suspension

  * ### Intro

    * *Suspension intro slide*  
    * The goal of the suspension is to:  
      * Maintain a good aero “platform” while sacrificing as little “mechanical grip” (i.e. vertical load variation and lateral load transfer) as possible  
      * Provide the driver a balance and ride quality which allows them to operate the vehicle at the limit of the tires capability  
    * Why do we have suspension at all?  
      * Consider a go-kart with no suspension  
        * Highly responsive on a very smooth surface  
        * Bumps, curbs, and surface transitions cause a big problem  
          * Tires come off the ground, terrible ride for driver  
          * We can’t have this at extremely high speeds  
      * Suspension allows us to overcome this problem  
        * Allows for influencing the behavior of the chassis/body (sprung mass) separately from the suspension near the wheel, or unsprung mass  
          * **Sprung mass** \- All mass supported by the suspension (chassis, engine, driver, body)  
          * **Unsprung mass** \- All mass that moves directly with the profile of the track (wheels, tires, hub, upright, brakes)  
        * Good suspension design allows the sprung mass to remain relatively still and stable while the unsprung mass moves rapidly to follow the profile of the road  
    * Inboard vs outboard  
      * *Suspension inboard vs outboard slide*  
      * Typically talk about suspension as inboard (chassis side) or outboard (wheel side)  
      * Example: a coil over has an outboard mount on the unprung suspension, and an inboard mount on the sprung chassis

  * ### Topology

    * *Suspension topology slide*  
    * **Suspension topology** \- the carefully arranged mechanical layout of linkages and force elements (springs and dampers) which connect our unsprung and sprung masses.  
      * Design and ability to adjust varies greatly depending on the series in which we are racing  
      * Front and rear topology will be at least slightly different (front has steering), and can be vastly different  
    * Review common examples across the top forms of motorsports  
      * Example: F1 (team designs everything)  
      * Example: IndyCar (spec series)  
      * Example: NASCAR Cup series (spec series)

  * ### Mechanical linkages

    * *Suspension linkage slide*  
    * Solid mechanical linkages and components which connect the force elements and sprung to unsprung suspension  
      * Examples  
        * Uprights, wishbones or control arms, rockers, push rods  
      * Design considerations  
        * Achieve specific kinematic behaviors based on placement of pivot or attachment points (camber curves, etc)  
        * Limit compliance   
        * Weight and packaging  
        * Aerodynamics  
      * Often there is some adjustability in the inboard/outboard mounting position of the mechanical linkages  
        * Trade off for adjustability is weight and packaging

  * ### Springs

    * *Springs slide*  
    * **Spring** \- compliant mechanical component that generates a force proportional to its displacement  
      * Store energy when compressed and release it when returned to an unloaded state  
      * Can work (i.e. be displaced) in heave, roll, or both  
      * Control how much the car moves  
    * Main types of springs are torsional vs translational  
      * Torsional springs generate force when twisted  
      * Translational springs generate force when compressed  
    * Common spring configurations used in motorsports  
      * Corner spring \- torsional or translational spring that displacements with suspension movement (typically in both heave and roll)  
        * Coil over is an example  
        * Called a “corner” spring because there is typically one per corner of the car  
      * Third spring \- translational spring that works only in heave  
        * Typically found in “formula” type cars like IndyCar or F1  
        * Displaced when rockers rotate in opposite directions (like they do in heave)  
      * Bumpstop \- translational spring, generally with a very “stiff” rate  
        * Used to stop the car from traveling in heave and making excessive contact with the ground  
        * Many topology options for where the bumpstop might be located  
          * Inside a corner damper  
          * On the shaft of a corner damper  
          * On a third spring/damper  
      * *Anti-roll bar slide*  
      * **Anti-roll bar (ARB, sway bar)** \- torsional spring which connects both sides of an axle and resists lateral body roll during cornering  
      * More complex than other springs, how they work:  
        * Can be a single piece or consist of a bar with arms  
        * Use “drop links” to connect the ARB to suspension   
        * Often the droplinks are compliant and introduce some non-linear rate to the “ARB system”   
        * *Review scenario on whiteboard*  
          * During cornering we build lateral acceleration and body rolls onto the outside tires   
          * If we freeze the car mid corner and rotate it from a front view to make the chassis “flat” (i.e. go from global to chassis frame) we would see the outside suspension is displaced upward and the inside tire has displaced downward relative to the chassis/body  
          * Intuitively, we know the wheels need to displace roughly the same amount in the same direction to keep the body flat (which is our goal with an ARB)  
          * Now imagine, anytime the outside wheel moves up, we could also force the inside wheel to go up thus limiting the roll? This is the idea behind an ARB.   
          * Connects to both sides of the suspension so that when one is displaced upwards, so is the other  
        * Can be a single piece (common in passenger cars) or consist of a bar with arms  
          * Both use “drop links” to connect the ARB to each side of the suspension  
          * Droplinks are often compliant to introduce some non-linear rate to the “ARB system”   
        * The tradeoff \- ARBs can provide a better aero platform but increase lateral load transferThe tradeoff \- ARB gives us a better aero platform but increased lateral load transfer  
      * Good example of typical tradeoffs we have to make as engineers as race engineers  
      * **Spring rate** \- amount of force or torque required to displace a spring a given amount  
      * Most common way to characterize a spring  
      * Can be defined as:   
        * Spring linear rate  
        * Table of displacement and associated force/torque  
      * *Spreadsheet of force/torque vs displacement*

  * ### Dampers

    * *Dampers slide*  
    * **Damper (shock)**\- hydraulic mechanical device designed to dissipate kinetic energy from suspension movement by converting it into thermal energy (heat)  
    * Controls how fast the suspension moves  
      * Mechanics  
      * As the damper is compressed or extended (rebound), a piston inside the body moves through a chamber filled with hydraulic oil  
      * The oil is forced through tiny, restrictive valves  
      * The faster you try to move the piston, the harder it pushes back (same principle as aerodynamics we studied before).  
      * *Pass around physical damper*  
    * The configuration and size of the values through which the oil flows is highly adjustable  
      * Shim stacks on a piston \- internal change that requires rebuilding  
      * Clicks on the body \-  external setting easily adjusted  
      * Adjustments can be made to in both or a single direction  
    * Characterization  
    * *Review damper curves*  
      * Dampers are measured by force shaft velocity curves / table  
        * Low-speed behavior controls low frequency movement caused (body roll during corner steering, pitch during braking, etc).  
        * High-speed behavior controls high frequency movement caused by the track surface (hitting a sharp curb, etc).  
        * Exercise on identifying different kinds of damper curves

  * ### Steering

    * *Steering system slide*  
    * **Rack and pinion steering** \- the steering wheel rotates a gear (pinion) that moves a horizontal toothed bar (rack) left and right. This pushes or pulls the steering arms attached to the front wheels and steers the car.  
      * By far the most common type of steering system used in auto racing.  
    * Power assistance  
      * Hydraulic power steering  
        * Uses hydraulic pressure to assist the movement of the rack as the pinion rotates  
        * System used by F1 and NASCAR Cup Series  
      * Electronic power steering  
        * Uses an electronically controlled motor to assist the movement of the rack  
        * Used in LMDh, WEC, and other sports cars   
      * No power assist  
        * Used in IndyCar and Formula E  
    * **Steering ratio** \- ratio of degrees of steering wheel angle turned by the driver to angle front wheels actually pivot  
      * Example: if you turn the steering wheel 15 deg and wheels actually pivot 1 deg, the ratio is 15:1  
      * Racing cars typically have faster ratios (maybe 8:1 to 14:1) as compared to passenger cars (15:1+)  
        * Allows for quicker corrections  
      * Very important characteristic of a steering system  
        * Set based on driver preference and track configuration  
    * *Mechanical linkage & ackerman slide*  
    * Mechanical linkage  
      * **Tierod (trackrod)** \- mechanical connection between the steering system on the upright  
        * Inboard and outboard pivot positions often adjustable  
        * Attachment at the upright is either in front of or behind the steering axis to allow for creating of moment to turn wheels  
        * **Ackerman** \- steering geometry principal that accounts for the inside front tire needing to turn at a greater angle (due to smaller cornering radius) than the outside tire

* ## Powertrain

  * *Powertrain slide*  
  * **Powertrain** \- system on a car that delivers energy from the power source to the track surface and controls the vehicle’s forward or backward motion  
  * Power sources  
    * Very complex system, we will focus on what concerns race engineers  
    * Internal combustion engine (ICE)  
      * Outside of Formula E, all mainstream race series use an ICE power source  
      * ICEs convert a petroleum based fuel into mechanical energy  
      * Fuel can only be added manually while the car is stopped  
      * Fuel burns thus reducing its weight and potentially shifting the spring CofG and mechanical balance  
        * F1 case:  
          * You carry enough fuel to complete the entire race without refueling (110kg, 140 liters, 37 gal)  
          * Car gets lighter as the race goes, typically run fastest laps towards the end of the race  
          * Since the fuel cell is near the CofG balance shift isn’t a huge concern  
        * NASCAR case:   
          * Must refuel multiple times throughout the race (only carry 20 gal, 75 liters)  
          * Fuel cell is just behind the rear axle (i.e. far from the CofG)  
          * Balance can change significantly due to fuel burn multiple times during the race  
    * Electric motor  
      * Series like F1 and IndyCar utilize an electric motor and ICE as power sources  
        * I.e. they are hybrids  
      * Batteries that power the motors are continuously recharged by “harvesting” kinetic energy while braking or off throttle  
        * This is a gross over simplification, the systems on different vehicles are highly specific and thus it is out of scope to cover here  
        * A lot study and optimization goes into the strategy around when during the course of a lap the energy is harvested and when it is deployed  
    * As a race engineer, we care about our power source’s performance relative to our competition (helps with tradeoff decisions) but there is not much we can do about it so it is not a big area of focus for us  
  * *Differential slide*  
  * **Differential (diff)** \- allows inside and outside drive wheels to rotate at different speeds while still delivering power from the power unit  
    * During cornering, the outside tires have to travel a greater distance than the inside tires, thus must rotate faster  
    * Race cars use tunable Limited-Slip Differentials (LSD) which limits the difference in rotation speed between the wheels to prevent sending all the power to the wheel with the least traction

* ## Brakes

  * *Disc brake slide*  
  * Modern race cars use disc brakes  
    * Slow car by clamping down brake disc which is fixed to hub  
      * Converts wheel/disc’s kinetic energy into heat using friction  
    * Brake caliper is mechanism used to clamp brake pads onto disc  
      * Contains pistons which push against the brake pad when driver presses brake pedal  
  * As a race engineer, the main things we care about are:  
    * Cooling the brakes  
      * Ducting configurations and required air flow to cool sufficiently  
        * Less air flow (opening) usually better for aero  
    * Adjusting the braking system for driver  
      * Typically don’t have many options for discs or calipers  
      * Can easily adjust the pad to change initial “bite” and feel of the brakes to the driver  
    * Brake bias   
      * Amount of braking done at front compared to rear  
      * Common adjustment to brake system (generally driver adjustable)  
      * Typical, most braking is done by front axle  
      * Impacts balance on entry to heavy braking corners  
        * More front bias \= more understeer  
          * Front tires generating more longitudinal force, less lateral

  * ### Experiments

    * In order to learn about the vehicle we have modeled, we need to subject our vehicle model to different virtual experiments  
    * **Vehicle dynamics simulation experiment** \- virtual testing of the vehicle model which mimics lab or track conditions and outputs data for engineering analysis  
    * *Static experiments slide*  
    * Static experiments  
      * Assembly / Assembler \- Kinematic assemble of virtual vehicle components in order to achieve wheel alignment targets and exercise vehicle in heave and roll  
        * Kinematic only, no force / moment balancing  
        * Inputs: virtual component definitions, wheel alignment and ride height targets  
        * Outputs: Shims, heave / roll results vs wheel travel (kin curves), element lengths  
        * *Review example kin graphs*  
      * Setup \- experiment that places the virtual vehicle at the prescribed setup state by tuning force elements, shims amounts, and CofG position to balance forces and achieve wheel alignment targets  
        * Force / moment balance returning results from a known state  
        * Input: results from assembler, setup targets, force element definitions, compliance values  
        * Outputs: Shims, spring element preloads, element lengths, CofG position  
        * *Review example of setup results*  
      * Static / Out of Garage / Pit Lane \- experiment that places the virtual vehicle at the prescribed static state by applying user defined conditions  
        * Force / moment balance returning results from an unknown state(s)  
        * Input: results from setup, user defined conditions  
        * Output: Loads, heights, element lengths, CofG position  
        * *Review example of static results*

      * *Speed-path-follow lap sim slide*  
      * **Speed-path-follow lap sim** \- virtual dynamic experiment in which the vehicle model follows a given trajectory at a given speed around a particular track  
        * Main experiment type for correlation to track data  
        * Can provide lap time sim sensitivity by evaluating available Fx and Fy and iterating on target speed trace  
        * Inputs: the defined vehicle model (typically results from OOG or pit lane state), trajectory and speed target, track model, driver model parameters

        * **Track model** \- digital representation of road surface created from scans of a physical track using in lap simulations  
          * Specific companies specialize in scanning and creation of tracks

      * *Optimization lap sim slide*  
      * **Optimization lap sim** \- lap sim experiment which seeks to optimize the vehicle’s path, speed, driver control, and/or setup by treating these inputs as free variables to be solved during the simulation  
        * Commonly used for EMS deployment/regen strategy  
        * Often simplified vehicle model  
      * *Cornering and straight-line sim slide*  
      * **Cornering sim** \- virtual dynamic experiment in which the vehicle model is run through a generically defined smooth surface corner at or through a specified speed range until reaching a given termination criteria  
        * Example: corner radius is 200m, 50-250 kph, 98% saturation or gLat=3

        * Outputs: time histories of all available data channels, calculated metrics

      * **Straight-line sim** \- virtual dynamic experiment in which the vehicle model is run in a straight-line on smooth surface through a specified speed range until reaching a given termination criteria  
        * Example: 50-350 kph, 8 gear  
        * Inputs: the defined vehicle model, experiment settings  
        * Outputs: time histories of all available data channels, calculated metrics

  * ### Experiment solve types

    * *Experiment type slide (with table)*  
    * Quasi-static \- simulation solve type that assumes changes happen infinitely slowly and thus acceleration and inertia terms are neglected  
      * Advantages: speed  
      * Disadvantages: reduced fidelity  
      * Experiment types: cornering, straight-line, lap sim, static  
    * Quasi-steady state \- assumes vehicle moves only at steady velocity or under steady operating condition thus steady-state centrifugal and centripetal forces are present, but transient acceleration dynamics are ignored  
      * Advantages: speed (as compared to fully dynamic)  
      * Disadvantages: reduced (as compared to fully dynamic)  
      * Experiment types: cornering, straight-line, lap sim, static  
    * Dynamic \- solves the complete set of time-domain differential-algebraic equations or ordinary differential equations of motion with no inherent simplifications  
      * Advantages: high fidelity  
      * Disadvantages: slow (compared to QS/QSS)  
      * Experiment types: Lap sim, DiL, setup experiment

* ## Balance

  * ### Theory review

    * *Balance review slide*  
    * **Understeer** (tight) – front tires slipping (less lateral capability) more than rears \- stable but can be slow  
    * **Oversteer** (loose) – rear tires slipping more than fronts \- less stable but potentially faster  
    * **Neutral steer** (neutral) \- neither understeer or understeer  
    * On track our driver will tell us the balance as they perceive it  
      * Varies depending on driver  
    * In simulation, a basic way to look at balance is comparing the tire slip angles between front and rear  
      * More slip in the front is understeer (tight)  
      * More slip in the rear is oversteer (loose)  
    * Overtime, we gain a feel for what sim predicted balance means on track  
    * We typically want the car just to the side of understeer  
      * Would rather err more stable given uncontrolled variables (weather)  
      * For a high HP car, we need to protect rear tires for corner exit

  * ### Metrics

    * **Lateral load transfer distribution (LLTD)** \- the axle based distribution of lateral load transferred from the inside to outside tires  
      * Generally expressed as a percent of distribution at the front axle  
      * Mechanical balance metric \- we can adjust with suspension  
      * Higher value \= more weight transfer at front \= more understeer  
    * **Aero balance** \- distribution of aero forces between front and rear of a car and their subsequent impact on the car’s balance  
      * Generally expressed as percent of aero load at the front axle  
      * Balance metric \- we can adjust with wing or body changes  
      * Higher value \= more aero load at front \= more

    * *Performance metrics slide*

      * Adding load to tires gives us more lateral capability

    * **Tire vertical force and variation \-**

* ## Data acquisition systems (telemetry)

  * *DAQ slide*  
  * **Data acquisition (DAQ)** \- system which records analog signals from physical sensor inputs and translates these signals into data for analysis  
    * Many sensors across car  
    * Looms connecting all sensors back to central computer  
  * *Sensors slide(s)*  
  * Common sensors  
    * Laser \- measures height to ground \-  ride height  
    * Linear potentiometers (pot) \- measures linear distance between two points \- shock or rocker pot  
    * Rotary potentiometers (pot) \- measures rotation angle \- steering shaft  
    * Accelerometer \- measures acceleration in one or more directions  
    * GPS position \- measures latitude, longitude, and elevation of the vehicle  
    * Pitot tube \- measures air speed  
    * Wheel speed sensor \- measures rotational velocity of wheels  
    * Temperature sensor \- measures heat  
    * Pressure taps \- measures air pressure \- typically in array  
    * Strain gauge \- measures deformation due to stretching or compression of suspension member \- converted into force  
    * ECU / SECU \- Driver controls, power unit, differential, and other control system related channels  
  * Different series have different rules regarding what DAQ can be done and when  
    * NASCAR is limited to only gps and ECU channels during race events, but can run additional sensors for testing  
    * F1 allows for running many sensors in addition to the SECU channels for all sessions  
  * Other concepts  
    * Setting gains \- All sensors will come with a documentation about the relationship between their change in state to output voltage which is read by the DAQ system  
      * Example: a damper pot may produce a voltage of 0 when fully extended and 5 when fully compressed.  
    * Zeroing \- concept of establishing a state of the vehicle in which various sensors on the car are at a value of “0” for the purpose of measuring displacement from this state  
      * Example: We may have a calculated channel for wheel travel that we want to read zero at the setup state of the car so we can tell how much the wheel has traveled in bump or droop from setup.   
    * Thermal drift \- behavior of sensors, especially strain gauges, can change significantly with heat  
    * Always question the DAQ system if something does not look right  
  * Typical DAQ setup workflow:  
1. Car is instrumented with sensor, looms, computers  
2. Gains for each sensor are input into DAQ software  
3. Car is put into “zero state” and sensors are checked/zeroed  
4. Car moves to another static state, maybe OOG with engine running  
5. Car goes on track and telemetry is streamed back or collected upon return
